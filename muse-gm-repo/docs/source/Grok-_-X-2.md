- Yale: Aristotle's causal engine and the production of fear

Yale's classical storytelling curriculum is built on Poetics, and the relevant mechanism is plot as causal structure (mythos), not as a sequence of episodes. Aristotle identified the patterns in successful tragedy that still govern contemporary screen narrative. The two tragic emotions—pity and fear—are not independent effects; they are produced by the structure:· Pity is generated when a character suffers undeservedly. · Fear is generated when the audience recognizes that the same causal chain could produce the same suffering in their own world.This is why "if-then" causation must be the spine of the Muse engine. Yale's framework also supplies reversal (peripeteia) and recognition (anagnorisis) as structural nodes: the moment the character (and audience) understands the causal truth is the moment fear crystallizes into dread.---

- Harvard: narrative empathy, suspense architecture, and visual externalization

Harvard's writing theory contributes three mechanisms the Muse engine must encode:Narrative empathy as a designed effect. - Where the inspiration for gangsters actually came from

The gangster genre’s raw material came from three converging historical forces, none of which were fictional invention:Prohibition (1919–1933) was the structural engine. The Eighteenth Amendment banned alcohol manufacture, sale, and transport, creating an illegal market that organized crime rushed to fill. The “razzle-dazzle of the Prohibition era” is what inspired Josef von Sternberg to make Underworld (1927), widely considered the first gangster feature. Without Prohibition, the bootlegger-archetype—the self-made outlaw who defies a corrupt system—would not have crystallized.Real crime reporting and trial coverage supplied the character templates. The Murder, Incorporated trials (1940–41) and the Kefauver Crime Committee hearings (1950–51) directly spawned gangster subgenres. Scarface (1932) was explicitly designed to exploit the real-life Chicago of Al Capone. Baby Face Nelson inspired a slew of gangster biopics. - Who wrote the scripts—and how they wrote them

Kishōtenketsu (起承転結) is a four-part narrative structure that originated in classical Chinese poetry and became a foundational form across East Asian storytelling in China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam. The Japanese name is the one most commonly used in English discussions; the Chinese equivalent is qǐchéngzhuǎnhé (起承轉合), the Korean is giseungjeongyeol (기승전결).

[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu)

It organizes material around development + contrast + recontextualization rather than escalating conflict as the primary engine.The Four Parts

Part

Kanji

Meaning

Function

Ki

起

Introduction / rise

Establish characters, setting, situation, and the baseline facts the reader needs.

Shō

承

Development / receive

Expand and deepen what was introduced. No major rupture; the frame still holds. Details accumulate, relationships clarify, the ordinary world becomes textured.

Ten

転

Twist / turn

The structural heart. An unexpected shift, new element, perspective change, or contrast that reframes everything that came before. It does not have to be a Western-style “plot twist” or climax of combat; it can be a revelation, a tonal break, a new character, a time jump, or an image that forces reinterpretation.

Ketsu

結

Conclusion / bind

Reconcile the first two parts with the ten. The pieces settle under the new light; meaning coheres. Often leaves a residual aftertaste rather than a pure “victory.”

A classic teaching example (attributed to Rai San’yō) illustrates the logic in miniature:

- Ki: The daughters of Itoya in the Honmachi of Osaka.

- Shō: The elder is sixteen, the younger fourteen.

- Ten: Throughout history, generals have killed the enemy with bows and arrows.

- Ketsu: The daughters of Itoya kill with their eyes.

The third line appears unrelated until the fourth line makes the connection spark.Origins and SpreadThe form began in Tang-dynasty (618–907) four-line regulated verse (jueju / qiyan jueju). Each line mapped to one stage. It later moved into prose, rhetoric, essays, and narrative fiction. In Japan it was adapted for yonkoma (four-panel comics), longer manga, essays, and fiction; Osamu Tezuka and other manga practitioners treated the four-panel unit as a natural home for it.

- Short-form versions (poetry, yonkoma) can function with very little external conflict.

The ten supplies the primary narrative charge through contrast and recontextualization.
